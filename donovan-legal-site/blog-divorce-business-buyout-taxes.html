THE BUYOUT NUMBER ISN’T THE NUMBER

TAX PLANNING · August 29, 2026 · By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA

The buyout number your lawyers are negotiating isn’t the number. The number is what’s left after the structure decides who pays the tax — and the structure is chosen, deliberately or by accident, in the drafting of the settlement agreement. Unlike almost everything else in tax, this is a choice the regulations hand to the parties. Most parties never learn they had it.

ROUTE ONE: THE §1041 TRANSFER, PRICED HONESTLY

The departing spouse transfers her interest to the keeping spouse for cash, other marital assets, or a note. Section 1041 makes the transfer itself tax-free — and hands the keeping spouse the old basis. Nothing was taxed; everything was deferred into the keeping spouse’s eventual exit. That deferral has a price the schedule should charge: an interest worth $3 million with $500,000 of inside basis is not a $3 million asset to the spouse who keeps it. If the buyout is paid over time, §453B(g) lets an installment note move between the spouses without triggering gain, imputed-interest rules under §§483 and 1274 will characterize part of every payment as interest whether the note says so or not, and the note itself — security, acceleration, remedies against a business the payor now controls alone — is the real protection. A promissory note between ex-spouses is exactly as good as its default provisions.

ROUTE TWO: THE REDEMPTION — WHERE THE ELECTION LIVES

The company buys the departing spouse’s stock. Two completely different tax stories can describe that transaction. In the first, the departing spouse sold stock to the corporation: her gain, capital, measured against her basis. In the second, the corporation made a constructive distribution to the keeping spouse — potentially a dividend — followed by a deemed §1041 transfer: his tax, at his rates, possibly without capital-gain character. Before 2003 the circuits whipsawed these facts — Arnes in the Ninth Circuit pointing one way, Blatt and its line in the Tax Court pointing the other, with the government free to argue against both spouses. Treasury ended the chaos with §1.1041-2: the tax falls by default on the redeemed spouse, unless a divorce or separation instrument, or a valid written agreement, expressly shifts it. In other words: the parties can pick, in writing, which spouse bears the redemption’s tax — a genuinely elective allocation of tax liability, sitting inside a family-law document, drafted by whoever knew it was there.

Two more layers for redemptions. First, §302 decides whether the redeemed spouse’s proceeds are sale proceeds or a distribution — and because §318 attributes stock between family members, a “complete termination” can be spoiled by attribution unless the redeemed spouse files the §302(c)(2) waiver: a formal agreement to acquire no interest (other than as creditor) for ten years, with notification requirements. Divorce is the classic setting where that waiver earns its keep. Second, the cash comes from the company: in an S corporation that interacts with stock basis, AAA and the §1368 distribution ordering; in a C corporation, with earnings and profits and the double-tax overhang the purchase price should have discounted and rarely did.

THE PARTNERSHIP OVERLAY: §736 AND THE HOT-ASSET TRAP

When the interest is in a partnership or an LLC taxed as one, the buyout speaks §736’s language: payments for the partner’s share of property under §736(b) produce capital treatment; payments characterized under §736(a) are ordinary — guaranteed payments or distributive shares — and deductible-in-effect to the continuing owners. The allocation between (a) and (b) is, again, substantially a drafting matter. And §751 sits underneath everything: if the partnership holds unrealized receivables or appreciated inventory, the “capital gain” buyout is partly ordinary income no matter what the agreement calls it. A professional practice — where receivables are the value — is the standard casualty.

THE §754 DIMENSION: WHEN THE BUYOUT MANUFACTURES DEPRECIATION

Where the interest is in a partnership holding appreciated depreciable assets — a real estate partnership being the standard case — a §754 election adds a second axis to the structure menu. The question is no longer only who pays the tax; it is whether the tax anyone pays manufactures basis the continuing side gets to use.

The pure §1041 route harvests nothing: the transfer takes gift treatment, and §743(b) adjusts inside basis only on a sale or exchange or a death — so even with the election in place, the keeping spouse inherits carryover basis outside and old basis inside. But a redemption in which the departing spouse recognizes gain converts that recognized gain, through §734(b), into a step-up of the partnership’s own asset basis — allocated under §755 toward the appreciated property, and where that property is depreciable, recovered as newly placed-in-service property. The buyout dollars come back as depreciation deductions against the very rents the keeping spouse now collects alone. A purchase by the other partners does the same work through §743(b), personal to the buyers. Run the numbers and the §754 step-up is frequently worth a meaningful slice of the buyout price — value that exists in one column of the structure table and simply doesn’t in another, and that neither family lawyer will have priced.

Diligence cuts both ways: confirm whether the election is actually in place (or can be made for the year of the transaction), model the §755 allocation before assuming the step-up lands on depreciable assets rather than land or goodwill, and remember the election is a ratchet — it forces step-downs too, and §743(b) reductions are mandatory for substantial built-in losses even without it.

THE VALUATION LAYER: WHERE THE GOODWILL SITS

Martin Ice Cream and its line hold that goodwill personal to the owner — the relationships, the reputation, the covenant not to compete — can be an asset distinct from the entity’s. In a divorce buyout that matters twice: it moves value out of the entity for the redemption analysis, and it changes what the marital estate even contains, since several states treat personal goodwill differently from enterprise goodwill in the division itself. The valuation expert’s allocation is a tax document. Someone with a tax license should read it before it hardens.

The structure is negotiable exactly once — before the marital settlement agreement is signed. The firm sits alongside your family lawyer for precisely this conversation, and writes the §1.1041-2 election the regulations will respect.

AND THE FEES BECOME BASIS

The valuation battle, the forensic accountant, the negotiation of the buyout itself — professional costs of acquiring or defending the interest are capitalizable into it under the same authorities that govern every acquisition. On a business worth fighting over, that is real basis almost nobody records. The fee-basis article linked below explains the half of the fee answer that gets left out.

FOR COUNSEL — THE AUTHORITIES

The redemption-vs-transfer architecture, and the drafting that controls it:

  • Treas. Reg. § 1.1041-2 — a divorce-related redemption’s tax burden is effectively assignable by the parties’ written instrument; absent that, default rules place it by constructive-distribution analysis.
  • Arnes v. United States, 981 F.2d 456 (9th Cir. 1992), and Blatt v. Commissioner, 102 T.C. 77 (1994) — the pre-regulation split that produced whipsaw results; the regulation exists because parties who didn’t choose let courts choose for them.
  • Craven v. United States, 215 F.3d 1201 (11th Cir. 2000) — this circuit’s contribution: § 1041 protection read functionally, not formally.
  • I.R.C. § 736(a)/(b) and § 751 — in partnership buyouts, the payment’s label decides ordinary-vs-capital, and hot assets convert “capital” buyouts into ordinary income.
  • Martin Ice Cream Co. v. Commissioner, 110 T.C. 189 (1998) — personal goodwill as a distinct asset; in valuation-driven buyouts, where the goodwill sits can restructure the deal.
  • I.R.C. §§ 453, 483, 1274 — deferred-payment buyouts carry installment and imputed-interest mechanics whether or not the note mentions them.
  • I.R.C. §§ 302(b), (c)(2), 318 — sale-vs-distribution treatment for the redeemed spouse; family attribution and the ten-year waiver that rescues complete-termination treatment in divorce redemptions.
  • I.R.C. § 453B(g) — buyout notes move between spouses without accelerating the deferred gain.
  • I.R.C. § 1368 — S corporation distribution ordering where the company funds the redemption.
  • I.R.C. §§ 754, 734(b), 743(b), 755 — the election converts a gain-recognizing redemption into an inside basis step-up allocated toward appreciated assets; § 743(b) does the same for purchasing partners. No adjustment arises on the § 1041 route — gift treatment is not a sale or exchange. Step-up increases to depreciable property are recovered as newly placed-in-service property.

Practice point: the § 1.1041-2 election is a drafting act. If the MSA is silent, the default rule is the client’s position — know it before signing, not at examination.
