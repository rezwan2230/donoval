Drop-and-Swap and Swap-and-Drop: The Sequencing the Tax Court Actually Looks At

By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA, CCM

The problem is old and the facts are always the same. A partnership owns a building. Some partners want to sell and take cash; others want to exchange into replacement property under § 1031 and keep deferring. A partnership interest is not real property and cannot be exchanged, so the partnership cannot solve the problem by having the departing partners exchange their interests. The solutions are the “drop and swap” — distribute undivided interests in the building to the partners, who then exchange (or sell) individually — and its mirror, the “swap and drop,” in which the partnership exchanges first and then distributes the replacement property. Both work. Both fail. The difference is in what the Tax Court looks at, and it is not what most closing checklists look at.

The statutory frame

Section 1031(a)(1) requires that the property given up and the property received both be “held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment.” Since 2018, only real property qualifies. A partnership interest was excluded expressly under the old § 1031(a)(2)(D) and is excluded today because it is not real property; the one surviving statutory concession is that an interest in a partnership with a valid § 761(a) election out of subchapter K is treated as an interest in each of the partnership’s assets. That sentence is the reason tenancy-in-common structures are drafted the way they are.

The holding requirement is tested for the taxpayer who does the exchange. In a drop and swap the exchanging taxpayer is the partner, and the government’s argument is that the partner acquired the undivided interest in the distribution for the purpose of exchanging it, not for investment. In a swap and drop the exchanging taxpayer is the partnership, and the argument is that the partnership acquired the replacement property to distribute it, not to hold it.

The cases, and the sequence they reward

Bolker. A corporation liquidated, distributing real property to its sole shareholder, who exchanged it the same day under a contract he had negotiated before the liquidation. The Tax Court and the Ninth Circuit held the held-for-investment requirement satisfied: the taxpayer did not intend to liquidate the investment or use the property personally, and “held for” does not require a minimum holding period. Bolker v. Commissioner, 81 T.C. 782 (1983), aff’d, 760 F.2d 1039 (9th Cir. 1985). The government had argued Rev. Rul. 77-337, which denied an exchange on nearly identical facts. The court declined to follow it.

Magneson. The taxpayers exchanged real property and, as part of the same plan, contributed the replacement property to a partnership. The Ninth Circuit held the exchange qualified: continuing the investment in a different form is still holding for investment. Magneson v. Commissioner, 81 T.C. 767 (1983), aff’d, 753 F.2d 1490 (9th Cir. 1985). Rev. Rul. 75-292, which denied an exchange followed by a contribution to a controlled corporation, again went unfollowed.

Chase. The counterexample. A partnership negotiated the sale of its building; immediately before closing it distributed undivided interests to the partners who wanted to exchange, and those partners exchanged. The Tax Court held the partners never held the interests for investment: the partnership had negotiated and controlled the sale, the distribution was a step in the partnership’s sale, and the partners were conduits. Chase v. Commissioner, 92 T.C. 874 (1989). The court reached the result on a substance analysis of who really sold, in the tradition of Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331 (1945).

Read together, the cases do not draw a line at the calendar. Bolker exchanged on the day of the distribution and won. Chase distributed before a closing the partnership had arranged and lost. The distinction is who negotiated and who is bound. A partner who receives an undivided interest and then, as owner, negotiates and contracts for the exchange holds the property for investment. A partner who receives an undivided interest in a property the partnership has already contracted to sell is executing the partnership’s sale.

The sequence that survivesDistribute first. Then let the co-owners, as co-owners, engage the broker, negotiate the contract, sign it, and open the exchange with the qualified intermediary. If the purchase and sale agreement is signed by the partnership, the drop is already too late.

What the government looks at now

The IRS abandoned litigating the pure timing question after Bolker and shifted to disclosure and to the co-ownership arrangement itself.

Form 1065, Schedule B. The partnership return carries two questions written for this transaction. Question 11 is a checkbox: whether, during the current or prior tax year, the partnership distributed any property received in a like-kind exchange or contributed such property to another entity — the swap-and-drop, with a two-year reach. Question 12 asks whether, at any time during the year, the partnership distributed to any partner a tenancy-in-common or other undivided interest in partnership property — the drop. A “yes” to either is a flag, not a disallowance. A “no” that is wrong is a different kind of problem, and preparers who answer these questions without asking are the most common way a drop and swap becomes an examination.

The co-ownership after the drop. If the former partners continue to operate the property jointly — shared bank account, one manager with authority to bind the others, profits shared other than by ownership percentage — the IRS will argue that the partnership never ended and the “undivided interests” are still partnership interests. Rev. Proc. 2002-22 sets out the conditions under which the IRS will rule that a tenancy in common is not a partnership: unanimous approval of sales, leases and financing; proportionate sharing of revenue, expenses and debt; no business activity beyond customary services; each co-owner’s right to partition and to transfer. It was written for the DST and TIC syndication market, but it is the checklist an examiner applies to a post-drop co-ownership. Meet it in the co-ownership agreement, and operate that way.

State conformity. Several states, California most aggressively, examine drop-and-swap transactions on their own account and are not bound by Bolker. For property in a state with its own like-kind regime the analysis has a second track.

Swap and drop

The mirror transaction — the partnership exchanges, then distributes the replacement property — presents the same question at the partnership level: did the partnership hold the replacement property for investment, or acquire it to distribute? Magneson supports the position that changing the form of the investment is not a failure to hold. The risks that are specific to this sequence are different. A distribution of the replacement property within the § 1031(f) related-party framework can trigger recognition if the replacement came from a related person. A distribution to a partner who contributed property within seven years raises §§ 704(c)(1)(B) and 737. And a distribution of encumbered replacement property shifts liabilities among the partners under § 752 in ways that can produce deemed distributions and gain before anyone has sold anything. The tax on the swap is deferred; the tax on the drop is a separate calculation.

The election that changes the analysis

A § 761(a) election, available to an unincorporated organization used for investment purposes (not the active conduct of a business) whose members can compute their own income without computing partnership income, takes the co-ownership out of subchapter K altogether and, by the express language of § 1031(a)(2), makes each member an owner of an undivided interest in the real property for exchange purposes. It is the cleanest structure for a group that intends to hold, exchange and split. It is unavailable to a group that operates the property as a business, and it must be in place — on a timely return — before the exchange, not asserted afterward.

The transaction is routine when it is planned twelve months out and litigated when it is planned at the closing table.
