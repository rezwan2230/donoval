Dividing Real Estate Under §1041: What Carries Over, What Doesn’t, and the One Asset That Cannot Move

By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA, CCM

Family lawyers know § 1041: a transfer of property between spouses, or between former spouses incident to divorce, produces no gain or loss, and the transferee takes the transferor’s basis. What the statute does not say is that the transfer carries a set of tax attributes with it, that several of them are worth more or less than the property itself, and that one of them — a deferred Opportunity Zone gain — is not carried at all but detonated. This note is written for the family-law bar: what moves with real estate under § 1041, what does not, and where the marital settlement agreement has to say something the Code will not say for it.

The mechanics that always apply

Basis. The transferee’s basis is the transferor’s adjusted basis, § 1041(b)(2) — carryover, even when fair market value is lower, which is the one respect in which a § 1041 transfer is treated less favorably than a gift. Reg. § 1.1041-1T, Q&A-11.

Holding period. Tacks under § 1223(2). A spouse who receives a building the couple bought twelve years ago has a twelve-year holding period the day after the transfer.

Depreciation and recapture. Accumulated depreciation is part of the adjusted basis that carries over, so the unrecaptured § 1250 gain and any § 1245 recapture on a cost-segregated building carry to the transferee and are taxed to the transferee on sale, at the transferee’s rates. A building with $3 million of value and $2 million of accumulated depreciation is not a $3 million asset to the spouse who receives it.

Debt. Section 1041 applies to encumbered property and the assumption of debt is not consideration. But a transfer of property subject to a liability that exceeds basis is still a § 1041 transfer with no gain — the statute overrides the general rule that relief of liabilities is an amount realized.

Timing. “Incident to divorce” means within one year after the marriage ends or, if later, “related to the cessation of the marriage,” which the regulations presume for transfers made under a divorce or separation instrument within six years. Reg. § 1.1041-1T, Q&A-6, -7. A transfer more than six years out is presumed not to qualify unless the presumption is rebutted. Settlement agreements that defer a real estate transfer should say why.

What carries, and what the agreement should price

Suspended passive losses. Section 1041(b)(1) treats the transfer as a gift for income tax purposes. Section 469(j)(6) then governs: on a disposition by gift, the transferor’s suspended passive losses attributable to the property are added to its basis in the transferee’s hands and are not deductible by the transferor. The spouse who kept the rental property that generated years of suspended losses gets those losses as basis; the spouse who transferred it loses them entirely. A settlement that divides the property without accounting for the suspended-loss balance divides the tax attributes unequally without knowing it.

Real estate professional status. Section 469(c)(7) status is determined per taxpayer per year. If one spouse qualified during the marriage and the couple filed jointly, the losses were current. After the divorce, the spouse who receives the property must qualify alone — 750 hours, more than half of personal services, material participation — or the property becomes passive to that spouse on day one.

Section 121 on the residence. Two rules in § 121(d)(3) save the exclusion for the spouse who leaves. The transferee’s ownership period includes the transferor’s under a § 1041 transfer, § 121(d)(3)(A). And a spouse who is out of the house but whose former spouse is granted use of it under a divorce or separation instrument is treated as using it as a principal residence during that period, § 121(d)(3)(B). The second rule is the one that gets missed: it lets the spouse who moved out five years before the sale still take the exclusion on his or her share if the decree gave the other spouse the use. The decree has to say it.

Installment obligations. A § 1041 transfer of an installment note from a prior sale is not a disposition that accelerates the gain, § 453B(g). The transferee reports the remaining payments as the transferor would have.

The attribute that does not carryA Qualified Opportunity Fund interest holding deferred gain is the one asset that cannot be moved between spouses without tax. Under Reg. § 1.1400Z2(b)-1(c)(3)(ii), a transfer between spouses or incident to divorce under § 1041 is an inclusion event: the transferor recognizes the deferred gain at the transfer, the ten-year exclusion clock ends, and the transferee holds an ordinary partnership interest with none of the OZ benefits. Section 1041 does not override it; the regulation was written to say so. A QOF interest has to stay with the spouse who owns it, be equalized with other assets, and be valued in the settlement net of the deferred gain that would come due if it ever moved.

Entities and the co-owned building

Most marital real estate of any size is held in an LLC. A transfer of the LLC interest is a § 1041 transfer of the interest, with the partner’s outside basis, capital account and share of liabilities carrying over; the transferee steps into the transferor’s § 704(c) position and, if the partnership has a § 754 election in effect, a § 743(b) adjustment is not available because there is no transfer for value. A redemption of one spouse’s interest by the LLC in exchange for a distribution of property, by contrast, is not a § 1041 transfer at all — it is a § 731 distribution, with its own recognition rules on cash in excess of basis and on marketable securities, and with §§ 704(c)(1)(B) and 737 in play if property contributed within seven years is involved. The two routes to the same economic result are taxed differently, and the choice belongs in the settlement.

Where the couple co-owns directly and one spouse buys the other out, § 1041 makes the buyout a non-event to both, and the buying spouse’s basis in the acquired half is the selling spouse’s basis, not the price paid. The price paid is simply not deductible and not basis. That result surprises buying spouses who financed the buyout and expected the loan to be a cost of the property.

Where the Code stops and the agreement starts

The Code allocates nothing between former spouses; it only tells each what he or she has. The settlement agreement is where the following are decided, and each is a number: the built-in gain and recapture carried with each property; the suspended-loss balance that moves as basis; the § 121(d)(3)(B) grant of use; the QOF interest that cannot move; the § 469(c)(7) status that will not survive the split; and the indemnity for tax on a joint-return year that is still open, which is its own subject. Family lawyers do not need to compute these. They need to know that each one exists, that each one is either priced or overlooked, and that a tax advisor brought in after the property schedule is negotiated is being asked to grade a paper that has already been handed in.
