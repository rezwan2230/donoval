How your state calculates child support

Why a tax lawyer belongs in the child-support conversation

The guideline worksheet answers one question: how much moves between the households each month. It is silent on the questions that decide how much of that money the family keeps. Who claims each child — and whether the claim alternates, which requires a signed Form 8332 every year the non-custodial parent claims. Who files head of household, a status that follows the child’s residence and cannot be traded away. Who gets the dependent-care credit and the dependent-care FSA, which belong only to the custodial parent. Who controls the 529 and who is the successor owner. How the self-employed parent’s income is characterized for support against how it is reported for tax. In Florida the credit allocation is a listed deviation factor; in Massachusetts the parties are required to consider it; everywhere it is negotiated at mediation by people who do not do tax.

Bring this schedule to the tax lawyer on your side of the table. The support number is the family lawyer’s; what surrounds it is ours.

The guideline sets the support. It does not decide who claims the child.

Child support itself is neither taxable nor deductible. The decisions around it — the child tax credit, head-of-household status, the dependent-care credit, the 529 — are worth thousands a year and the guideline worksheet allocates none of them. Your divorce lawyer’s engagement letter says to consult tax counsel on exactly this. Bring the estimate to the tax lawyer on your side of the table — retained by you, so your lawyer’s file stays clean and yours gets the advice.
