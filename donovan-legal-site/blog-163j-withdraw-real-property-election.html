§163(j) After OBBBA: Should Your Real Estate Partnership Withdraw Its Election Out?

By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA, CCM

Between 2022 and 2024, thousands of leveraged real estate partnerships elected out of the § 163(j) business-interest limitation as an “electing real property trade or business” under § 163(j)(7)(B). The election was irrevocable, and it carried a price: the alternative depreciation system for the partnership’s nonresidential real property, residential rental property and qualified improvement property, and with ADS the loss of bonus depreciation on QIP. When the election was made the price was cheap. The limitation was being computed on an EBIT basis — depreciation no longer added back to adjusted taxable income after 2021 — and bonus depreciation was phasing down toward zero. For a property with a mortgage and a depreciation schedule, electing out was often the only way to deduct the interest.

Both premises are gone. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act restored the depreciation, amortization and depletion add-back to ATI for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2024, and made 100% bonus depreciation permanent for property acquired after January 19, 2025. Rev. Proc. 2026-17 (March 18, 2026) then did something the Code does not: it let taxpayers withdraw an election made for a taxable year beginning in 2022, 2023 or 2024, as if it had never been made. The window closes on the earlier of October 15, 2026 or the end of the period of limitations on assessment for the election year — and the second date is the one that bites. The revenue procedure’s own examples make the point: a calendar-year 2022 election on a return filed March 1, 2023 had to be withdrawn by April 15, 2026, and that date has passed; a 2022 return filed on extension in September 2023 has until September 2026, which is now. For most 2022 elections the door is closed or closing this month. The 2023 and 2024 elections have until October 15. This note is about whether to use it.

What the withdrawal does

A taxpayer that made the election on a timely filed original return for a year beginning in 2022, 2023 or 2024 may withdraw it by filing an amended return for the election year with “FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2026-17” at the top and the prescribed withdrawal statement attached, together with conforming amended returns for every affected later year (section 4.01). On withdrawal the taxpayer is treated as never having elected (section 4.02): the trade or business is back inside § 163(j), depreciation for the affected property is redetermined under § 168 with the basis adjusted accordingly (section 4.03), and bonus depreciation becomes available for property that was denied it — including QIP placed in service in the election year and after. The change is made on the amended returns, with any § 481 adjustments modified, not through an accounting-method change. A taxpayer under examination hands the agent a copy no later than the day it files (section 4.04).

Two features of the procedure matter for partnerships. First, a BBA partnership is not confined to an administrative adjustment request: section 7 lets an eligible BBA partnership that filed its 2022–2024 returns before March 18, 2026 file an amended Form 1065 with amended Schedules K-1 instead, and the partners then amend their own returns as “affected taxpayers” under section 4.01(2)(b). That is usually the cleaner route where the partners are few and current; an AAR is the route where they are many, or where the partnership prefers to pay an imputed underpayment rather than send amended K-1s to former partners. Second, a withdrawing taxpayer may make a late § 168(k)(7) election on the same amended return (section 5) — that is, elect out of bonus for a class of property the withdrawal made bonus-eligible. A partnership whose partners cannot use a large passive loss in the election year can withdraw the election, recover the interest deduction, and still spread the depreciation.

The withdrawal is all-or-nothing for the election and the trade or business, and the partnership’s capital accounts have to be restated to reflect it or they cease to be maintained under § 1.704-1(b)(2)(iv) (section 4.02). It is not a change of method that can be timed; it is a retroactive erasure with a filing deadline.

The arithmetic that has changed

Three numbers decide the question for a given partnership.

Interest capacity under EBITDA. With depreciation added back, 30% of ATI is 30% of something close to net operating income before debt service. For most stabilized real estate, that is more than the interest on the debt. A property that could not fit its interest under an EBIT limitation in 2023 will often fit it comfortably under EBITDA in 2025 and after, and disallowed interest carries forward indefinitely rather than being lost.

The depreciation the election cost. ADS lives are longer (30 years residential, 40 nonresidential) and QIP under ADS is 20-year straight-line with no bonus. A partnership that renovated tenant space in 2023 or 2024 under the election has QIP on a 20-year schedule that, without the election, would have been 15-year property eligible for whatever bonus percentage applied in the placed-in-service year — 80% for 2023, 60% for 2024 — and, for property acquired after January 19, 2025, 100%. Withdrawal recovers the difference on the amended returns.

The go-forward election value. The election out remains available for future years, and remains irrevocable once made. A taxpayer that withdraws now is not choosing “never elect”; it is choosing to decide again on the 2025 return with EBITDA and permanent bonus in the calculation. For a heavily leveraged development or a value-add asset in lease-up, where interest is high and income is not yet there, the election may still be right. For a stabilized asset, it usually is not.

Where withdrawal is usually wrongA partnership with a large 2022–2024 interest disallowance carryforward that it will not use for years, little QIP, and a debt load that still exceeds 30% of EBITDA. Withdrawal would reinstate a limitation it cannot clear and would recover little depreciation. Leave the election in place.

The partner-level complications

Section 163(j) at the partnership level. The limitation is computed by the partnership, and disallowed interest becomes excess business interest expense allocated to the partners under § 163(j)(4), carried at the partner level and deductible only against excess taxable income allocated from the same partnership. Withdrawal for 2022–2024 creates that allocation retroactively for each partner for each amended year, and each partner’s own returns must reflect it. For a partnership with turnover in its partners since 2022, the amended K-1s go to former partners.

BBA partnerships. Amended Form 1065 under section 7, or an AAR under § 6227 — the partnership chooses, subject to the same October 15 outer date and, for the AAR, the § 6227(c) period. A partnership under examination may use the amended-return option only after written notice to the revenue agent.

Depreciation recapture and § 1250. The recovered depreciation is ordinary to the extent of § 1250 recapture and unrecaptured § 1250 gain on a later sale. The withdrawal does not change the total; it moves deductions earlier and gain character later. For a partner planning a near-term sale or a § 1031 exchange, that timing is the analysis.

The passive-activity overlay. Accelerated depreciation recovered on amended returns is a passive loss for a partner who does not materially participate, suspended under § 469 until there is passive income or a disposition. For a real estate professional under § 469(c)(7) with an aggregation election in place, it is deductible. The cash value of withdrawal differs by partner for the same partnership.

A decision sequence

For each partnership that elected in 2022, 2023 or 2024: (1) recompute the § 163(j) limitation for each election-era year on an EBITDA basis to see whether the election was ever needed; (2) quantify the ADS-versus-GDS and bonus differential on QIP and on property placed in service during the election years; (3) model the partner-level effect of the retroactive excess business interest allocation and the § 469 character of the recovered depreciation for the principal partners; (4) decide, and file the amended returns or AAR by the earlier of October 15 and the assessment-limitations date for the election year, which for a 2022 election filed on extension is September 15. For a partnership that is a candidate, the work is a few weeks. The deadline may be less.
