How your state sets alimony

Why alimony is a tax question first

Before 2019 the payor deducted alimony and the recipient paid tax on it, which moved income from a high bracket to a low one and made a larger payment cheaper for both sides. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act ended that for every instrument signed after December 31, 2018. Now the payor funds the payment from after-tax dollars and the recipient keeps it whole — federally. Massachusetts, New York and California did not follow, so in those states the old regime still runs on the state return. The statutory percentages in Massachusetts and New York were written for the old world; whether they should be read down for the new one is an argument at every mediation.

The trade-off section prices the stream against a lump of property. Cash and Roth balances replace it dollar for dollar; appreciated stock and a pre-tax 401(k) carry their own tax and need to be grossed up. That is the negotiation, and it is the conversation to have with the tax lawyer on your side of the table.

The alimony number is a tax number now.

Since 2019 the payor pays alimony from after-tax dollars and the recipient keeps it tax-free — except in Massachusetts, New York and California, where the state still deducts and taxes it. Every dollar of support is worth something different to each side, and a property offset is worth something different again. Your divorce lawyer’s engagement letter says to consult tax counsel on exactly this.
