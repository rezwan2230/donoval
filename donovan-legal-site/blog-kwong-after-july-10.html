Kwong After July 10: The Appeal, the Action on Decision, and Who Still Has a Claim

By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA, CCM

The July 10, 2026 date that dominated the first half of this year has passed. For most taxpayers, it was the last day to file a protective refund claim measured from the extended deadline that Kwong v. United States, 179 Fed. Cl. 382 (2025), read into § 7508A(d). This note is about what is true on the other side of that date: where the litigation stands, what the government has committed to in writing, which taxpayers may still have an open door, and what a claimant who filed on time should now expect.

What Kwong held, in one paragraph

The pre-November 2021 version of § 7508A(d) provided that, for a “qualified taxpayer” affected by a federally declared disaster, the period beginning on the earliest incident date and ending 60 days after the latest incident date “shall be disregarded” in determining whether specified acts were timely — the same acts the Secretary may postpone under § 7508A(a). The Court of Federal Claims held the provision self-executing and mandatory, held that the COVID-19 declaration ran from January 20, 2020 through the end of the emergency on May 11, 2023 (so the disregarded period ran to July 10, 2023), and invalidated the Treasury regulation that had capped the mandatory period. On that reading, failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties and underpayment interest accruing inside the window were not properly assessed, and refund-claim and refund-suit limitation periods were correspondingly extended.

The government's position, in three documents

The appeal. The Department of Justice filed its notice of appeal to the Federal Circuit on May 15, 2026. Briefing will run into 2027. A decision either way is likely to be sought at the Supreme Court given the dollars involved.

The Action on Decision. On May 20, 2026, the IRS issued an Action on Decision on Abdo v. Commissioner, the Tax Court case that first read the 2019 statute to require a mandatory 60-day postponement. The AOD acquiesces in the narrowest possible way — the IRS accepts that the statute compels a minimum 60-day postponement from the declaration — and states that the Service will not agree to any interpretation extending mandatory relief beyond that. That is a formal instruction to examiners and Appeals: Kwong-based claims are to be disallowed pending the appeal.

The lookback fix. The Disaster Related Extension of Deadlines Act, P.L. 119-64 (December 26, 2025), added § 7508A(f): for purposes of the § 6511(b)(2)(A) lookback, “any period disregarded under this section with respect to the time prescribed for filing any return of tax shall be treated as an extension of time for filing such return.” It applies to claims filed after the date of enactment — which is every Kwong claim filed this year. The practical effect: a timely claim is no longer limited to amounts paid within three years plus the ordinary six-month extension; the lookback reaches back through the disregarded disaster period, so payments made during the postponement are recoverable. The same Act amended § 6303(b) so that the notice-and-demand clock respects a disaster postponement. Neither provision speaks to whether the disaster period was in fact “disregarded” under the 2019 statute — that remains the question on appeal — but if Kwong is affirmed, the lookback is no longer an obstacle to collecting on it.

Who still has a door open

July 10, 2026 was the three-years-from-filing date only for returns treated as filed on July 10, 2023 under Kwong’s reasoning. Section 6511(a) is disjunctive: a claim is timely if filed within three years of the return or within two years of payment, whichever is later. Three groups should be looked at now rather than written off:

Late payers. A taxpayer who paid a disaster-period penalty or the interest on it after September 4, 2024 — on an installment agreement, through a levy, by offset of a later refund — is inside the two-year prong today, and the amount recoverable under the lookback is the amount paid within those two years.

Filers whose return was actually filed late. If the 2019, 2020, 2021, or 2022 return was itself filed after its original due date, the three-year prong runs from the actual filing date, and for many that date is in 2024 or 2025.

Open-year taxpayers. Anyone under examination, in Appeals, or in collection for a disaster-period year can raise Kwong as an affirmative position without a refund claim at all — in a protest, a Collection Due Process hearing, or a Tax Court petition. The AOD tells the IRS to disagree; it does not prevent the issue from being preserved.

What a timely protective claim buysA protective claim filed by July 10 preserved the taxpayer’s position; it did not resolve it. Expect a disallowance letter citing the AOD. That letter starts the two-year clock under § 6532(a) to file a refund suit — unless the taxpayer and the IRS execute a Form 907 extension, which is the routine way to keep a claim alive while the appellate court decides. Calendar the disallowance date when it arrives; do not let § 6532 run while waiting for the Federal Circuit.

Our practical read

The taxpayer-side reading of the 2019 statute is strong on the text and has now been adopted by two courts. The government’s best arguments are that “in the same manner” incorporates the Secretary’s discretion under subsection (a), and that a three-and-a-half-year nationwide postponement is a result Congress could not have intended — the same argument the Court of Federal Claims rejected, but one that reads differently at the appellate level and after the 2021 amendment that Congress made to fix the drafting. We would not build a client’s cash planning on a Kwong refund. We would preserve every claim that costs little to preserve, extend every disallowance by Form 907, and raise the issue in any open controversy where it moves the number.
