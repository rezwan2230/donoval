YOUR LAWYER SAID CONSULT TAX COUNSEL

Somewhere in your divorce lawyer’s engagement letter is a sentence like this: “We do not provide tax advice. You are advised to consult tax counsel.” It is there for a good reason — family lawyers do family law, and the tax consequences of a divorce are a different practice. But nobody tells you who that counsel is, or what they do, or when to call. This page does.

A high-net-worth divorce is a business reorganization

A forced unwind of a jointly owned economic unit: title, basis, entities, debt, deferred compensation, real estate, carryforwards — and who eats the tax when the paper 50/50 hits a 1031, a QOF, an S corporation or a recapture clause. The children are usually already handled by the time the money is on the table. The fight is the cap table, and the cap table is tax. This is the same firm that reads the loan documents, the operating agreement, the K-1 and the return, applied to a marital balance sheet.

What a tax lawyer does in a divorce that no one else at the table does

Your family lawyer negotiates the division and drafts the agreement. A forensic accountant, if there is one, values the assets and may testify. Both work at gross value, and both stop where the tax begins. The questions that decide what you actually keep — what a 401(k) dollar is worth next to a Roth dollar, whether the house keeps its $500,000 exclusion or loses half of it, whether the spouse keeping the business inherits the whole tax bill, who claims the children, who is liable for the joint returns already filed, where the loss carryforwards go — are tax law, and they live in the language of the settlement agreement. That language is what we write.

You retain us directly. Not through your lawyer, and not as your lawyer’s expert. A separate engagement means your lawyer’s tax disclaimer stays intact, you get advice from someone who answers only to you, and the two of us coordinate with your permission. If a case needs a forensic accountant later, we know exactly what to ask them to prove — most cases never get there.

Six questions, six free tools — in the order the reorganization goes

The first five are the unwind: what the estate is worth after tax, what the business is worth in your state, what happens to the real estate and the retirement accounts, and where the tax attributes go. The sixth is the tax year the deal closes in. Each tool runs in your browser, in your state (Florida, Massachusetts, New York, California or Texas), with the law summarized on the page and every assumption shown. Nothing you enter leaves your device. They are educational; the point is that you arrive at the table knowing which of these is worth money in your case.

1. Is the “equal” split equal after tax?

The Tax-Effected Marital Balance Sheet puts the embedded tax on every line of the forensic’s schedule and recomputes the equalization payment.

2. What is the business worth, in your state?

Fundamentals first, then the four fights — standard of value, goodwill, tax-affecting, discounts — and a draft Calculation of Value report.

3. What happens to the house?

Sell before the decree, buy your spouse out, or co-own and sell later — three paths priced after tax, and the one clause worth $250,000.

4. How do the retirement accounts divide?

Every account after tax, the vehicle each needs, the penalty exception only one carries, and the cash-out trap priced three ways.

5. Where do the loss carryforwards go?

The regulations decide, not the agreement; what each attribute is worth in each spouse’s hands, and how to equalize.

6. How do we file this year, and who is liable for the joint years?

December 31 decides the year; the three scenarios, and the § 6015 exposure on every joint return already filed.

Two edge instruments. For the case that turns on support, the firm keeps an alimony estimator and property trade-off and a child support estimator by state. In this kind of case they are rarely where the money is, and they do not lead.

And the document that ties them together: the Tax Rider to the Settlement Agreement — the tax chapter your agreement is missing, assembled from your facts as a schedule your family lawyer attaches to the agreement they drafted.

The engagement: a Divorce Tax Review

A fixed-fee engagement, quoted before you commit, scaled to the estate. We read the marital balance sheet the way the tools do and tell you what it says after tax; we identify the decisions in your case that carry tax consequences — the house, the business, the retirement accounts, the filing year, the children, the carryforwards — and give you and your lawyer the tax terms the agreement needs, in writing, as a rider ready to attach; and we take the call with your lawyer if you want us to. Where a business needs a value for mediation, the firm can issue a signed Calculation of Value report under the AICPA’s valuation standards, as an alternative to a forensic engagement most cases do not need. If the case goes to trial, we hand off to a forensic expert with a brief that tells them exactly what to prove.

How it works with your lawyer

  • Separate engagement. Our engagement letter runs to you. It states that we are not associated with your family lawyer, share no responsibility for their work, and divide no fee — and that you authorize us to speak with them.
  • Their file stays clean. Your lawyer never retains, vouches for or reviews our tax work. Their disclaimer means what it says, and so does our engagement.
  • We coordinate. With your permission we deliver the tax terms to your lawyer as a rider, join the settlement conference on the tax points if asked, and answer their questions — as your counsel, not theirs.
  • The forensic accountant is not displaced. If your case has one, we work from their schedule. If it does not, we may be the reason it does not need one.
