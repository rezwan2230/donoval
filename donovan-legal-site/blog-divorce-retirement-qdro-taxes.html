THE QDRO ISN’T THE FINISH LINE

TAX PLANNING · August 29, 2026 · By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA

The QDRO got signed and everyone went home. The tax treatment of what happens next is where retirement money actually gets lost — because the order divides the account, and a series of unglamorous mechanical choices, made in the weeks afterward by whoever answers the plan’s phone, divides the tax.

WHAT “QUALIFIED” ACTUALLY REQUIRES

A domestic relations order becomes a QDRO only when it satisfies §414(p): it must specify the plan, the participant and alternate payee, and the amount or percentage (or a determinable formula); it cannot require the plan to pay benefits the plan doesn’t offer or amounts already assigned to an earlier order. The plan administrator — not the judge — determines qualification, and during that review §414(p)(7) requires the plan to segregate the disputed amounts for up to eighteen months while the parties fix defects. An order the administrator rejects divides nothing; a settlement term that depends on it is a term that doesn’t exist yet. The competent sequence is to pre-clear the draft with the administrator before the decree, not to discover the plan’s model-order requirements after it.

The order should also answer the questions nobody wants to think about: what happens if the participant dies before segregation (survivor treatment under §414(p)(5) needs to be drafted, not assumed); whether the alternate payee is treated as surviving spouse for the plan’s pre-retirement death benefit; what happens to earnings and losses between the valuation date and the actual division. A share fixed as a dollar amount and a share fixed as a percentage behave very differently in a moving market — and the difference is somebody’s money, allocated by whichever default the drafter didn’t negotiate.

THE TAX MECHANICS, IN THE ORDER THEY BITE
  • Who is taxed: under §402(e)(1)(A), the spouse or former-spouse alternate payee is the distributee — distributions under the QDRO are her income, not the participant’s.
  • The penalty exception: §72(t)(2)(C) exempts QDRO distributions from qualified plans from the 10% additional tax. There is no IRA version. The under-59½ spouse who needs cash takes it from the plan under the order — taxable but penalty-free — and rolls only the remainder. Roll first and withdraw later, and the 10% is back. Order of operations, worth exactly ten percent.
  • Withholding: an eligible rollover distribution paid to the payee rather than directly rolled carries mandatory 20% withholding under §3405(c). A spouse who wants $100,000 in hand and asks for $100,000 gets $80,000 and a withholding credit; the direct-rollover-then-planned-distribution route avoids the surprise.
  • IRAs are a different statute: §408(d)(6) divides IRAs by transfer incident to divorce — retitling pursuant to the instrument, no QDRO. Outside that mechanism the movement is a distribution taxed to the owner, decree or no decree; Bunney is the standing warning. And the §72(t) exception never applies to IRA withdrawals, whatever the divorce context.
NOT ALL DOLLARS IN THE PLAN ARE THE SAME DOLLAR

A modern 401(k) is three tax regimes wearing one statement: pre-tax dollars (fully taxable on distribution), Roth dollars (tax-free if qualified, with a five-year clock whose history matters), and after-tax basis recovered under §72’s rules. Employer stock adds a fourth: net unrealized appreciation under §402(e)(4), where a lump-sum distribution of the stock itself is taxed only on plan basis, with the appreciation waiting as capital gain — a treatment a reflexive rollover permanently destroys. Outstanding plan loans make someone’s share smaller than the statement says. Which spouse takes which sleeve is a negotiable term with real spread — a Roth dollar is not a pre-tax dollar — and the schedule shows one number per account.

Ten minutes of tax counsel before the order is drafted routinely outperforms a year of argument about the split percentage.

WHAT A QDRO CANNOT TOUCH

Nonqualified deferred compensation — SERPs, elective deferrals, phantom equity — sits outside §414(p) entirely. It divides by contract in the settlement agreement, within the employer plan’s terms and §409A’s rigidity about time and form of payment, and under Rev. Rul. 2002-22 the transferee spouse is taxed when the deferred amounts pay out, with FICA and withholding mechanics that the agreement should assign. In executive divorces the nonqualified layer is routinely the largest number on the schedule — and the one the QDRO everyone worked so hard on never reaches.

FOR COUNSEL — THE AUTHORITIES

The statutory spine of the retirement division:

  • I.R.C. § 414(p) — what makes an order “qualified”; an order the administrator rejects divides nothing.
  • I.R.C. § 402(e)(1)(A) — the alternate-payee spouse is the distributee, taxed on plan distributions under the QDRO.
  • I.R.C. § 72(t)(2)(C) — the early-distribution penalty exception for QDRO distributions from qualified plans; it has no IRA analogue, so the rollover-first path forfeits it.
  • I.R.C. § 408(d)(6) — IRAs divide by transfer incident to divorce, not QDRO; outside that mechanism the transfer is a distribution to the owner. See Bunney v. Commissioner, 114 T.C. 259 (2000).
  • I.R.C. § 402(e)(4) — net unrealized appreciation on employer securities: a lump-sum/basis-only opportunity a reflexive rollover destroys.
  • I.R.C. § 409A — nonqualified deferred compensation sits outside § 414(p) entirely; it divides by contract, within the plan’s and the statute’s rigidity.
  • I.R.C. § 414(p)(5), (p)(7) — survivor treatment is a drafted term; the plan segregates disputed amounts for up to 18 months during qualification review.
  • I.R.C. § 3405(c) — mandatory 20% withholding on eligible rollover distributions paid to the payee rather than directly rolled.
  • Rev. Rul. 2002-22 — NQDC divided in divorce is taxed to the transferee at payout; § 409A constrains any change to time or form.

Practice point: draft the QDRO to specify the sleeve (pre-tax / Roth / after-tax basis), the valuation date, and earnings drift — the administrator’s default answers are nobody’s negotiated answers.
