FOREIGN ASSETS, FEDERAL TRAPS

TAX PLANNING · August 29, 2026 · By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA

The divorce is in Florida. The account is in São Paulo. The penalties for handling that wrong start in five figures per year — and the divorce itself is about to put the account under oath, in a proceeding your spouse’s counsel controls.

TWO REGIMES, PRECISELY

The FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) arises under the Bank Secrecy Act, 31 U.S.C. §5314: a U.S. person with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign financial accounts aggregating over $10,000 at any moment in the year must file. “Financial interest” reaches accounts owned through entities the person controls — more than 50% of a foreign corporation or partnership pulls its accounts onto your FBAR — and signature authority alone triggers filing for accounts you don’t own, including the account your parents added you to a decade ago. The filing rides its own calendar (April 15, automatically extended to October 15) and never touches the Form 1040.

Form 8938 is the tax-code twin under §6038D, filed with the return, reaching foreign financial assets more broadly — accounts, but also foreign stock and securities held directly, interests in foreign entities, and foreign-issued instruments — at higher thresholds that vary by filing status and residence (starting around $50,000 for an unmarried domestic filer). The regimes overlap without excusing each other: one asset can require both filings, each with its own penalty, and the FBAR’s Schedule B question sits on the 1040 either way, collecting admissions.

THE PENALTY MATH, POST-BITTNER

For non-willful FBAR violations, the Supreme Court held in Bittner (2023) that the penalty applies per unfiled report, per year — not per account. That tamed the nightmare arithmetic, but an inflation-adjusted five-figure penalty per open year is still real money for paperwork. For willful violations the statute bites per account, per year, at the greater of a six-figure floor or 50% of the account balance — and the case law (Bedrosian, Williams) treats reckless disregard as willful, with the checked “No” on Schedule B serving as the government’s favorite exhibit. Meanwhile §6501(c)(8) holds the assessment statute on the entire return open until required foreign information returns are filed, and §6501(e)(1)(A)(ii) gives the government six years where more than $5,000 of foreign income was omitted. Unfiled foreign forms don’t age off. They accumulate.

AND THE ASSETS THEMSELVES CARRY TRAPS
  • Foreign mutual funds: almost always PFICs under §1291 — a punitive interest-charge regime that can consume most of the gain, and a valuation reality the settlement schedule should reflect before anyone “wins” those assets.
  • Foreign pensions: treaty-dependent deferral, possible employer-trust reporting, and sometimes Forms 3520/3520-A with penalties starting at 35% of contributions/distributions and 5% of trust assets — a “retirement account” label from another country settles nothing.
  • Foreign entities in the division: interests moving between spouses can trigger Forms 5471/8865 filings neither return has ever carried.
  • §1041(d): the nonrecognition rule is switched off for transfers to a nonresident-alien spouse — the cross-border division that looks tax-free domestically can be a gain-recognition event outright.
  • The house abroad: foreign real estate held directly isn’t an 8938 asset — but the entity or account that holds it usually is, and the rental income was always reportable, with foreign tax credits nobody claimed.
WHY THE DIVORCE CHANGES THE CLOCK

Financial affidavits, mandatory disclosure, and forensic tracing document every account — under oath. The streamlined procedures that cure non-willful noncompliance at modest cost (three years of amended returns, six years of FBARs, a 5% or 0% penalty depending on residence) require a certification of non-willfulness — a statement your client makes once, in writing, on facts the discovery file will either support or destroy — and practically require that the government hasn’t already learned of the accounts. An angry spouse with a completed affidavit is how the government learns of accounts. The cheap fix has a window, and your own case schedule is what closes it. Simpler failures have simpler cures — delinquent FBARs with no unreported income can often be filed with an explanation and no penalty at all — but choosing the path is the legal judgment, made under privilege, before the affidavit.

If there are unreported foreign assets anywhere in the marriage, the conversation belongs under privilege, before the affidavit is signed — the options only narrow from there.

THE ORDER OF OPERATIONS

Inventory first — every account, entity, pension and property, both spouses, under privilege, with the Kovel accountant inside the tent. Cure second — through the disclosure path the facts support. Divide third — with the transfer reporting, the PFIC discount, and the §1041(d) analysis drafted into the agreement. In that order, this is compliance work with a known price. Backwards, it is penalty defense with your own affidavit as Exhibit A.

FOR COUNSEL — THE AUTHORITIES

The reporting regimes and the penalty math, precisely:

  • 31 U.S.C. §§ 5314, 5321(a)(5) — the FBAR obligation and penalty structure; FinCEN Form 114 at a $10,000 aggregate threshold.
  • Bittner v. United States, 598 U.S. 85 (2023) — non-willful penalties apply per report, per year — not per account. Willful penalties remain per account, at the greater of the statutory floor or 50% of the balance.
  • Bedrosian v. United States, 912 F.3d 144 (3d Cir. 2018), and United States v. Williams, 489 F. App’x 655 (4th Cir. 2012) — recklessness satisfies willfulness; the Schedule B checkbox is evidence against the filer.
  • I.R.C. § 6038D — Form 8938, separate statute and separate penalties; § 6501(c)(8) holds the entire return’s assessment statute open until required information returns are filed.
  • I.R.C. § 6501(e)(1)(A)(ii) — omitted foreign income over $5,000 extends the statute to six years.
  • I.R.C. § 1041(d) — no nonrecognition on transfers to a nonresident-alien spouse: the cross-border division can be a recognition event.
  • I.R.C. § 1291 — foreign mutual funds as PFICs; the interest-charge regime that should discount those assets on the schedule.
  • Forms 3520/3520-A — foreign trust and pension reporting, with 35%/5% penalty exposure independent of any tax due.
  • 31 C.F.R. § 1010.350 — financial interest and signature authority, including the >50% entity attribution that pulls company accounts onto a personal FBAR.

Practice point: sequence privilege before disclosure — the streamlined certification of non-willfulness is a statement your client makes once, on facts your discovery file will either support or destroy.
