WHAT FAMILY LAWYERS ASK US MOST

TAX PLANNING · August 29, 2026 · By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA

Family lawyers don’t hate tax. They hate owning tax answers they can’t verify, in cases where every number is contested and every mistake has their name on it. So the questions arrive carefully — and the same ones arrive again and again. Here are the recurring ones, answered the way we answer them, followed by the two that would change your cases if asked earlier.

“IS ANY OF THIS DEDUCTIBLE?”

As a current deduction, no — Gilmore’s origin-of-the-claim test killed the deduction for fees whose origin is the marital relationship, and §67(g)’s elimination of miscellaneous itemized deductions buried the §212 remnants. But the question the profession stopped asking is whether the property-related fees are capitalizable: the same Gilmore Court expressly reserved that question at 372 U.S. 52, the remand answered it by putting the same fees into basis, and the current regulations make capitalization of defense-of-title and acquisition costs mandatory, with appraisals, negotiation, and transaction tax advice on the inherently-facilitative list. Nothing on this year’s return; real money at the client’s eventual sale — if the allocation evidence exists, which is where your billing entries come in below.

“IS THE ALIMONY DEDUCTIBLE?” — THE QUESTION WITH A DATE ON IT

Not anymore, for any divorce or separation instrument executed after December 31, 2018: TCJA repealed §215, so alimony is neither deductible to the payor nor income to the recipient. Pre-2019 instruments keep the old regime — unless a post-2018 modification expressly opts into the new one, which makes every modification of an old support order a small tax election hiding in a family-law motion. The planning consequence runs through everything: without the deduction, the payor’s appetite for support shrinks and property-division dollars, retirement sleeves, and basis-rich assets become the currency of settlement. The tax planning moved from the support column to the property column. That is, frankly, why this practice exists.

“WHO CLAIMS THE CHILDREN?”

The custodial parent, by federal default under §152(e) — measured by nights, not by the decree — unless she releases the claim on Form 8332, which the noncustodial parent must attach year by year or multi-year. The decree saying “father claims the children in even years” is unenforceable against the IRS without the executed release; it is enforceable between the parties in your court, which means the agreement should obligate the execution, not just recite the outcome. Credits split along their own lines (the child tax credit follows the dependency; the dependent-care credit and head-of-household status stay with the custodial parent regardless), so the “alternating years” handshake is worth different amounts to each side — price it, don’t just alternate it.

“WHICH SPOUSE SHOULD TAKE THE RENTAL?” / “WHAT DOES THE BUYOUT DO TO BASIS?” / “SHOULD THEY FILE JOINTLY?”

The rental: the spouse who can use the depreciation, understands the §469(j)(6) suspended-loss position riding with it, and holds on a horizon that matches the recapture story — a facts question with a tax answer, worked in Equal on Paper. The buyout: whatever the structure says — §1.1041-2 lets a properly drafted instrument choose which spouse bears a redemption’s tax, the election closes at signing, and if you ask one tax question per case, ask this one before the MSA is drafted. The joint return: price both filings, then treat the signature as what it is — joint and several liability — and charge for it: indemnity, review rights, written refund allocation, and remember the §6013(b) asymmetry (separate can become joint later; joint is forever). Each has a full article in this section.

THE TWO QUESTIONS THAT CHANGE CASES

“What is this settlement worth after tax?” Asked at schedule assembly — not after offers are exchanged — it moves real money between the columns and hands you leverage the other side hasn’t priced. A face-value schedule is an opening position wearing a closing document’s clothes; a tax-adjusted column beside it is the cheapest expert work in the case.

“How should our time entries describe the property work?” Because fees defending or dividing property can enter asset basis, the billing narratives your firm writes this month are the allocation evidence your client’s CPA will need years from now. “Analysis of rental portfolio valuation for equitable distribution; conference with appraiser re: Building A” supports capitalization, asset by asset. “Attention to file” supports nothing. The discipline costs five minutes at entry and preserves a benefit someone else will discover a decade out — with you on record as the lawyer who caught it. (Entry specificity and privilege coexist: describe the asset and activity, not the advice.)

The firm works alongside matrimonial counsel — never as counsel of record, never touching the client relationship, on fixed fees your client sees before we start. Your case, our lane.

THE MECHANICS, FOR THE SKEPTICAL
  • Engagement: special counsel for the tax issues, scoped in writing; you remain sole counsel of record and sole owner of the relationship.
  • Privilege: where the forensic accountant’s work is tax-sensitive, a Kovel engagement routes the accountant through counsel so the analysis stays privileged — an architecture built at retention, not asserted at deposition.
  • Timing: the highest-value entry point is asset-schedule assembly; second, MSA drafting; third — and most expensive — after signing, when the elections have closed.
  • Fees: fixed and disclosed in advance. Contingent fees are barred in this work under Circular 230 §10.27 anyway — you and your client will always know the price.

FOR COUNSEL — THE AUTHORITIES

For the file, the authorities behind the five short answers:

  • United States v. Gilmore, 372 U.S. 39 (1963) — origin-of-the-claim kills the deduction; the Court expressly reserved capitalization at 372 U.S. 52. On remand, the same fees entered basis: Gilmore v. United States, 245 F. Supp. 383 (N.D. Cal. 1965).
  • Woodward v. Commissioner, 397 U.S. 572 (1970) — costs of acquiring or defending title are capital, whatever the litigation posture.
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.263(a)-2(e), (f)(2)(ii) — defense-of-title capitalization, and the inherently facilitative list: appraisals, negotiation, tax advice on the acquisition, document preparation.
  • I.R.C. § 67(g) — miscellaneous itemized deductions eliminated; capitalization is the characterization left standing.
  • United States v. Kovel, 296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961) — route the forensic accountant through counsel at retention; privilege is an architecture, not a label.
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.1041-2 — the redemption election answered in Question Three, supra.
  • TCJA § 11051 (repealing I.R.C. § 215) — no alimony deduction/inclusion for post-2018 instruments; pre-2019 instruments keep the old regime unless a modification opts in. Every modification is a tax election.
  • I.R.C. § 152(e); Form 8332 — dependency follows nights unless released in the prescribed form; the decree binds the parties, not the Service.
  • I.R.C. § 6013(b) — the one-way amendment door behind the joint-filing answer.

Practice point: billing narratives are the allocation evidence. “Analysis of rental portfolio valuation for equitable distribution” supports capitalization; “attention to file” supports nothing. Entry discipline this month is your client’s basis file next decade.
