There are three things to do with the house, and each has a different tax bill. Sell it now, before the decree, and the $500,000 joint exclusion is still available — once. One spouse keeps it, takes the other’s basis under § 1041, and inherits the entire built-in gain with only a $250,000 exclusion left; the buyout should be priced on that, not on gross equity. Keep both names on the deed and sell later, and the departing spouse loses the exclusion after three years unless the agreement contains the one sentence § 121(d)(3)(B) rewards. This tool prices all three, after tax, in your state.

Enter the home, the mortgage, the basis, and each spouse’s post-divorce income. The later-sale paths take a horizon and an appreciation rate; the buyout takes a refinance rate.

How your state treats the house

Why the house is a tax question before it is a housing question

Section 121 excludes $250,000 of gain per spouse, $500,000 on a joint return, if each has owned and used the home as a principal residence for two of the five years before the sale. Section 1041 makes a transfer between spouses incident to divorce tax-free — and gives the recipient the transferor’s basis, so the gain does not go away; it changes hands. Put those two rules together and every decision about the house has a number attached: the closing date that keeps or loses the joint exclusion, the buyout price that does or does not account for the tax the keeper inherits, and the sentence in the agreement that lets the spouse who moved out count the other spouse’s years of use. None of it appears on a forensic accountant’s schedule.

Bring this schedule to the tax lawyer on your side of the table. The buyout can be paid in other assets from the marital balance sheet rather than cash, which is where this tool and the balance-sheet tool meet.

One sentence in the agreement is worth a $250,000 exclusion.

The house is the asset most divorces fight over and the one most settlements get wrong on tax: a joint exclusion lost to a closing date, a buyout at gross equity that hands one spouse the whole tax bill, a departing spouse who loses the exclusion because nobody wrote the grant-of-use clause. Your divorce lawyer’s engagement letter says to consult tax counsel on exactly this.
