EQUAL ON PAPER, UNEQUAL AFTER TAX

TAX PLANNING · August 29, 2026 · By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA

You’re splitting $4 million evenly. If one side takes the brokerage account and the other takes the rentals, you are not splitting $4 million evenly — and the settlement schedule, which prices everything at appraised value, is structurally incapable of showing you the difference.

Start with the statute doing the silent work. Section 1041(a) makes transfers between spouses — or to a former spouse “incident to the divorce” — nonrecognition events, and §1041(b) treats the transfer as a gift for basis purposes: the recipient takes the transferor’s adjusted basis, whatever the property is worth. “Incident to the divorce” is itself defined generously: a transfer within one year of the marriage ending qualifies automatically, and a transfer within six years pursuant to the divorce or separation instrument is presumed related to the cessation of the marriage. So virtually every asset moving in a property settlement moves with its history attached — the basis, the depreciation schedule, the holding period, the built-in tax. The divorce doesn’t tax the appreciation, and it doesn’t cleanse it either. It assigns it, silently, to whichever spouse takes the appreciated asset.

A WORKED EXAMPLE, ALL THE WAY THROUGH

The estate is $4 million: a $2 million brokerage account with $1.4 million of basis, and a $2 million rental building with $1.1 million of adjusted basis, reflecting $350,000 of straight-line depreciation. Each spouse takes one. The schedule calls it 50/50. Now sell both, hypothetically, at those values the day after the decree:

Amount realized$2,000,000$2,000,000
Adjusted basis$1,400,000$1,100,000
Gain$600,000$900,000
CharacterLong-term capital gain$350,000 unrecaptured §1250 gain (25% ceiling) + $550,000 LTCG

After-tax proceeds≈ $1,857,200≈ $1,768,300

Illustrative rates including net investment income tax; state tax excluded. An "equal" division that is roughly $89,000 unequal on day one — before counting the depreciation stream.

Same face value, roughly $89,000 apart on an immediate sale — and that is the simple version. Spouse B’s building also throws off depreciation deductions against the rents every year she holds it, which Spouse A’s account never will; her remaining depreciable basis keeps sheltering income. And if she holds until death, current law hands her heirs a §1014 fair-market-value basis: the $900,000 of built-in gain, recapture story and all, simply evaporates. Horizon changes the answer. The spouse selling next year and the spouse holding twenty years should never price these assets identically — and in a properly advised division, they don’t.

THE ATTRIBUTES THAT RIDE ALONG

Basis is only the first passenger. Several more travel with the assets, each capable of moving the real division:

  • Suspended passive losses. Years of rental losses trapped under §469 do not spring free on a §1041 transfer; under the gift-transfer rule of §469(j)(6) they are added to the transferee’s basis instead. A property carrying $200,000 of suspended losses is a different asset in the hands of the spouse who could have used them against other passive income than in the hands of the one who can’t.
  • The residence and §121. The exclusion is $500,000 on a joint return and $250,000 for the now-single seller. A marital home with $700,000 of gain is fully sheltered if sold before the decree and leaves $450,000 exposed if the keeping spouse sells after it — though §121(d)(3) lets the out-spouse’s ownership period and a decree-based use arrangement preserve exclusion eligibility on a later joint-proceeds sale. Sequencing the home sale against the decree date is a five-minute conversation worth up to six figures.
  • Installment notes. Ordinarily, transferring an installment obligation triggers the deferred gain — but §453B(g) switches that rule off for §1041 transfers: the recipient spouse simply steps into the note and reports the payments as received. The gain follows the note; price the note accordingly.
  • Equity compensation. Under Rev. Rul. 2002-22, nonstatutory options and nonqualified deferred compensation transferred incident to divorce are taxed to the transferee when exercised or paid — assignment-of-income doctrine yields to §1041. The employee-spouse’s unvested and unexercised positions are divisible, and the tax lands on whoever takes them, at ordinary rates, with withholding mechanics that need drafting.
  • Accrued income. §1041 shields gain, not income already earned: under Rev. Rul. 87-112, interest accrued to the transfer date on transferred bonds stays taxable to the transferor. The line between appreciation and accrued income is exactly where sloppy schedules leak.

This is the analysis the firm runs at the settlement table — a tax-adjusted schedule set beside the face-value schedule, before any offer is exchanged and while the mix can still be chosen.

THE QUESTIONS THAT PRICE AN ASSET PROPERLY
  • What is its adjusted basis — and how much gain, of what character, with what recapture, is built in?
  • What does it do while held — produce sheltered income, throw off deductions, or consume carrying costs?
  • What is the realistic disposition — sale, exchange, or hold-until-death — by whom, in what bracket, on what horizon?
  • What rides along — suspended losses, exclusion eligibility, holding period, accrued income, withholding obligations?

Run those four on every line and the “equal” division usually moves, sometimes by six figures. The spouse who models after-tax value chooses assets. The spouse who doesn’t, accepts them — and learns what they accepted in the April after the first sale.

FOR COUNSEL — THE AUTHORITIES

The framework for pricing the division after tax:

  • I.R.C. § 1041(a), (b)(2) — no gain or loss on transfers incident to divorce; transferee takes transferor’s adjusted basis. The tax is deferred into the recipient, not forgiven.
  • I.R.C. § 1(h)(6) — unrecaptured § 1250 gain taxed at up to 25%; the depreciation history transfers with the building under § 1041.
  • I.R.C. § 121(b) — the exclusion drops to $250,000 for the now-single seller; § 121(d)(3) preserves use-periods for the out-spouse under a decree.
  • I.R.C. § 469(j)(6) — on a gift-type transfer, suspended passive losses are not freed; they are added to the transferee’s basis. A § 1041 transfer follows this pattern, changing which spouse should want the property.
  • Rev. Rul. 87-112, 1987-2 C.B. 207 — § 1041 does not shield accrued but unrecognized ordinary income; the transferor is taxed on interest accrued to the transfer date. Face value hides character.
  • I.R.C. § 453B(g) — § 1041 transfers of installment obligations are not dispositions; the transferee spouse steps into the note.
  • Rev. Rul. 2002-22, 2002-1 C.B. 849 — nonstatutory options and NQDC transferred incident to divorce are taxed to the transferee at exercise or payment; assignment-of-income yields to § 1041.
  • Treas. Reg. § 1.1041-1T(b), Q&A-6, -7 — the one-year rule and the six-year presumption defining “incident to divorce.”

Practice point: build the tax-adjusted schedule as a parallel column, not a memo — mediators use what they can see.
