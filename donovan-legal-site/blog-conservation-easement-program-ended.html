The Easement Settlement Program Is Over. What "You Can Still Ask" Actually Means

By Paul K. Donovan, JD, CPA, CCM

On August 19, 2026, the IRS ended the uniform conservation-easement settlement initiative it had announced on May 13 (IR-2026-65) and, in the same release, created an Office of Conservation Easements (IR-2026-95). No further uniform settlement letters will issue. The Service’s stated reason is instructive: standardized, unsolicited letters on a rolling basis, each with a fixed response period, “are not well suited to the full range of conservation easement cases,” because partnership agreements, insurance arrangements, and procedural posture differ in ways that affect when and how a taxpayer can evaluate settlement. That is a concession the practitioner bar has been making for two years. It changes the tactics for every case still open.

What the May program was

IR-2026-65 offered a tiered resolution for taxpayers in easement disputes under § 170(h): concede the deduction in exchange for a reduced penalty, with the penalty rate stepping up the longer the taxpayer waited and no upfront payment required for a large group of docketed cases. The IRS reported over 1,100 open matters at the time — roughly 740 docketed in the Tax Court and 400 in examination — and extended the offer to cases that had rejected or let lapse earlier initiatives. Our analysis of the terms is here; the terms are unchanged, the delivery mechanism is gone.

What the August release does

Ends the letters. Effective August 19, the IRS stopped issuing uniform settlement letters under the May framework. A taxpayer that received a letter before that date and is still inside its response period is unaffected; the offer stands until its stated expiration.

Keeps the terms available on request. A taxpayer with a pending case may continue to request settlement on the May terms through the assigned examination or Chief Counsel representative. If the case remains eligible, the IRS will issue an offer on the same standardized terms. The Service was explicit that this “does not signal a new or more favorable standardized offer.”

Preserves individualized resolution. Cases may continue to be resolved on different terms where the hazards of litigation warrant — the ordinary Appeals and Counsel settlement standard that the uniform program had, in practice, displaced.

Centralizes the function. The Office of Conservation Easements will coordinate policy, enforcement and case-resolution strategy across the IRS and with Chief Counsel, serve as the channel for general inquiries once operational, and work with Treasury on administrative and legislative options for § 170(h). Contact details are to be announced separately; until then, case-specific matters stay with the assigned representative.

The operative changeUnder the May program the IRS came to the taxpayer with a clock. Now the taxpayer goes to the IRS without one. Nothing forces a request, nothing expires, and the standardized terms are the floor a taxpayer can always fall back to — which means the only reason to accept them is that the case cannot do better on the hazards.

What “same standardized terms on request” is worth, case by case

The IRS’s own explanation for ending the program is the reason the standardized terms fit some cases badly. Three fact patterns illustrate it.

The insured partnership. Many syndicated easement partnerships purchased tax-liability insurance or have indemnity rights against the promoter. A standardized concession of the full deduction with a fixed penalty may be worse than litigation for the partners if the policy responds to a litigated loss but not to a voluntary concession, or if the promoter indemnity is triggered only by an adjudicated disallowance. The settlement decision is a coverage question before it is a tax question.

The docketed case with a valuation record. The government wins most easement valuation cases, but not all, and the margin of loss varies enormously. A partnership with a credible appraisal, a defensible highest-and-best-use analysis, and no Oakbrook or Hewitt deed defect has litigation hazards that a uniform 10-to-20% penalty does not reflect. Hazards-based settlement through Appeals or Counsel is the right venue, and the August release restores it.

The exam case with a deed problem. Where the deed fails the perpetuity requirement — a judicial-extinguishment clause of the kind rejected in Oakbrook Land Holdings, or an amendment clause that lets the parties alter the restriction — the deduction is lost on a ground that has nothing to do with value. For that case the standardized terms are a gift, and the request should go in now, before the case is docketed and before the Office of Conservation Easements begins to rationalize offers across the inventory.

Procedure from here

For a case in examination: the request goes to the revenue agent in writing, citing IR-2026-95 and asking for an offer on the May framework terms, or for referral to Counsel for hazards settlement if the case does not fit. For a docketed case: the request goes to the assigned Chief Counsel attorney, and the Tax Court’s pretrial schedule is the practical deadline. In both settings, the partnership-level mechanics that made the May program difficult — partner consents, the treatment of penalties at the partner level, the interaction with the BBA centralized audit regime — have not changed and still have to be resolved before a settlement can be executed.

The larger signal is that the IRS has moved from a campaign posture to an institutional one. An office that exists to coordinate § 170(h) enforcement and to advise Treasury on legislation is not a sign that the issue is winding down. It is a sign that the Service expects to be litigating easements for another decade and wants the results to be consistent. Taxpayers should expect the same consistency to be applied to them.
